Games: Fire Elblem: Shadow Dragon and the Blade of Light (FE1), Fire Emblem Gaiden (FE2), Fire Emblem: The Blazing Sword (FE7), Fire Emblem: The Sacred Stones (FE8)

In the article The Case for Video Game Tutorials, Assassin's Creed game designer Patrice Désilets states “The real story of a game is teaching how to play it. Everything else is noise.” (Schwerin, 2021). Unlike other forms of media such as books or films, video games are interactive experiences that require not only physical controls to be taught to players, but also how to engage with different elements of the gameplay. Consequently, tutorials are an integral part of video game design, although approaches between games can vastly differ.

First released in 1990, Fire Emblem is an extensive series of strategy role-playing games published by Nintendo. As of 2023, there are seventeen mainline games launched on Nintendo systems, spanning the Nintendo Entertainment System to the Nintendo Switch. Despite experimenting with mechanics over the course of thirty years of history, every game in the series contains the same core gameplay: not unlike chess, players move units of different classes across a grid-like map and can attack enemy units within range. Once all of the player's units have acted, the player's turn ends and the enemy’s phase begins. The series additionally regularly introduces and refines mechanics such as the emblematic Weapon Triangle Advantage system. Consequently, the franchise contains extremely varied level design as these grid-like maps must effectively tell a story in addition to providing engaging gameplay. With seventeen games worth of map designs, Fire Emblem provides a compelling look at how different entries in the series approach designing tutorials for similar mechanics despite technological limitations.

In an analysis of 32 popular FPS games that the authors determined to be representative of the genre, Benvenuti et al. (2023) developed a classification framework of 14 parameters for assessing tutorial design. The authors deduced that the most popular combination of parameters, found across 10 games, included: presence of tutorial, context sensitivity, help availability, text usage, voice usage, helping avatar, story integration, command scheme customization and controller diagram. The first 4 of the original 14 parameters, tutorial presence (whether the game has a tutorial or not), context-sensitivity (whether instructions appear before the game or are incorporated directly into the game and appear in-context), freedom (are players locked into completing certain actions before continuing), and availability of help (can players access help at any time), were drawn from Andersen et al.’s study, “The impact of tutorials on games of varying complexity” (2012). To evaluate the effectiveness of these parameters, Andersen et al. designed and tested three puzzle games with varying levels of complexity and different applications of the aforementioned principles. Based upon the results, the authors offered four main conclusions: “Tutorials were only justified in the most complex game”, “ Context-sensitivity can improve engagement”, “Tutorial freedom did not affect player behavior”, and “On-demand help harmed and helped player retention”. In other words, tutorials have a greater impact on improving player performance and retention in more mechanically complex games. Six subsequent parameters in Benvenuti et al.’s framework were drawn from Paras’s study “Learning to play: the design of in-game training to enhance videogame experience” (2006). This study analysed tutorial implementation into a hockey video game by comparing three conditions: control (no instructions besides controller layout diagram), before-play (instructions on all aspects of the game are presented before being allowed to play), and in-play (context-sensitive instructions). The author found that, while players in the in-play group performed better than those in the control group, there was a significant negative correlation between enjoyment and number of times users saw the instructions.

Furthermore, Cao & Liu’s study, “Learning to play: understanding in-game tutorials with a pilot study on implicit tutorials” (2022), introduces the concept of explicit versus implicit design when evaluating in-game tutorials. The authors developed two versions of the same game, one where the mechanics were taught by pop-up instructions (explicit), while the other had background objects depict arrows to show players what buttons to press in different situations (implicit). While participants rated the implicit implementation of the tutorial less boring than the explicit tutorial, they also rated it less helpful. Summarily, tutorial design varies significantly across games, with even simple parameters, such as how textual instructions are presented, impacting player engagement.

Given the complexity of designing methods to effectively teach players controls and mechanics, several papers have been dedicated to isolating common design strategies across games. Originally developed in 2008, the Game Approachability Principles (GAP) are a set of guidelines designed to assist game developers in the creation of effective tutorials (Desurvire & Wiberg, 2015) by drawing upon research in the fields of Human-Computer Interaction and User Experience. At the heart of the GAP is the concept of approachability, which the authors define as “making games initially more friendly, fun, immersive, and accessible” (169), specifically in the case of first-time players. Consequently, the GAP takes inspiration from psychological theories of learning to evaluate the effectiveness of different methods of teaching skills to players. The 2015 edition of the GAP consists of eleven principles (Figure 1).

Figure 1. The Game Approachability Principles

In contrast to Desurvire & Wiberg’s focus on approachability, Poretski & Tang’s 2022 paper, “Press A to Jump: Design Strategies for Video Game Learnability”, instead highlights the importance of learnability. While the authors acknowledge the usefulness of approachability to easing players into a game, they subsequently state that “ approachability cannot describe the learning aspects that need to support gameplay beyond the first 20 minutes: new and more advanced game controls, mechanics, and systems that gradually open themselves to the players” (3). In other words, learnability is, in the context of video games, all aspects throughout a game that help players gain proficiency with their mechanics. Consequently, Poretski & Tang developed a framework of eleven design strategies for learnability (Figure 2). Notably, Poretski & Tang divide their framework into three sections, demonstrating how learnability can exist outside of the gameplay itself.

Figure 2. Framework of design strategies for learnability

Using the frameworks provided by both Desurvire & Wiberg (2015) and Poretski & Tang (2022), this paper will utilise The Game and Interactive Software Scholarship Toolkit (GISST) to analyse how developers design the first levels in four different entries in the Fire Emblem series of games to effectively teach players the mechanics.

Fire Emblem: Shadow Dragon and the Blade of Light (FE1)

Fire Emblem: Shadow Dragon and the Blade of Light (FE1) was originally released in 1990 for the Nintendo Entertainment System. As the first game in the Fire Emblem series, FE1 had the task of introducing players to mechanics that would become series staples. While the included manual1 provides direct instructions on navigating menus and what different visual elements represent, it does not actually impart to players the skills needed to complete the game successfully. Consequently, the first map (Figure 3) is carefully designed not only to provide the player with a safe area for experimentation, but also to guide the player towards making smart moves without interrupting their play with direct instruction.

Fire Emblem Manual
Figure 3. Chapter 1: Marth Embarks

Throughout the series, Fire Emblem uses the term ‘objective’ to refer to the win condition for each map. Objectives can range from defeating all enemies on the map, defeating a boss, to surviving a certain number of turns. As the first entry in the Fire Emblem series, FE1 only makes use of two objectives: Seize (accomplished by occupying a castle tile on a map and selecting the requisite command) and Defeat the Boss. While future games would directly state the objective as a part of the UI, FE1 instead utilises the Seeding in the Cutscene strategy to introduce players to their goal. At the beginning of the map, several of the playable character discuss the inciting action of the game’s story - a group of pirates has attacked Talys, the island kingdom which has been sheltering main character Marth after the hostile takeover of his country of Altea2 . After being told the situation by the senior knight, Jagen, and Princess of Talys, Caeda, [1] Marth ends the cutscene by stating “Pirates are no match for us! Let’s head back to the castle to rescue the king!”. Players are thus informed of their goal (rescuing the king) organically without any need for extraneous instructions.

[2] First maps are a special case in Fire Emblem as this is the one time when designers know exactly what units the player is using in addition to their stats and equipment. In other words, they are able to design maps with the knowledge of exactly how much damage each unit will deal. This is exemplified in the character of Jagen, a paladin and the player’s sole promoted unit. Jagen has access to two weapons, an Iron Sword and a Silver Lance, both of which have specific use cases. The Silver Lance has the power to defeat any enemy (aside from the boss) in a single round of combat. [3] On the other hand, using the Iron Sword will reliably leave an enemy unit with 2 HP [4] , the perfect amount to let another unit safely get the kill and receive a significant amount of experience. This dynamic persists in Fire Emblem: Mystery of the Emblem (FE3) whose first map is a remake of the first game’s. In FE3, Jagen’s sole starting weapon is a Silver Lance. However, units have been balanced so that, once again, Jagen will leave most enemies with a minimal amount of HP [5] round of combat.[6] Thus, Jagen’s role is clear: He is to be used to either fully remove an enemy from play, or to weaken enemies so the player’s other units can safely defeat them. Furthermore, as a paladin, Jagen’s range of movement is the greatest among the player’s starting units and he starts closest to the enemy units. Consequently, his starting placement allows him to reach the nearest fort (a tile that grants the unit occupying it combat bonuses), making him the only character that can do so. This is an example of the Invisible Hand principle, where the player is guided by cues directly placed in the game world. In other words, Jagen’s placement naturally guides the player to use him, the safest unit to experiment with, to first engage with enemies.

Priests, another staple class in Fire Emblem and the preeminent healers, are often introduced very early in the game. However, rather than include a healer as one of the player’s starting roster, FE1 has the player recruit a priest towards the end of the map. By visiting the final village on the left side of the map, the priest Wrys will join the player’s party [7] . At this point in the map, players will have almost certainly defeated all enemies except for the stationary boss and the majority of their units will have taken damage. Thus, Wrys’ importance to the gameplay is emphasised and players will have the chance to heal before tackling their first boss. Priests are not able to heal units that have full HP, so introducing Wrys any earlier would simply leave players with a unit unable to take any action other than move. Introducing Wrys late also helps keep him safe as players experimenting with movement on the first turn will not be able to immediately put him in danger.

A further important aspect of Fire Emblem map design is pressure. Often, there will be some sort of mechanic that encourages players to not play slow and defensively. This first map introduces such a mechanic when the player ends their first turn [8] . The Computer’s turn will see one enemy pirate on the left side of the map move down to occupy a village, while a thief on the right side will move up from an already destroyed village towards another one. This is akin to the Demonstration of Type principle in the GAP, where players are shown an example of a mechanic to encourage a certain response. The thief’s movement teaches the player several things. One, that villages can be targeted by enemy units, and two, thieves will prioritise pillaging villages over attacking the player. Even if you leave the thief alone for the first turn, they will not be able to reach the village, so even players unfamiliar with how thieves work will have time to deal with them on the second turn.

Despite being the first map in the entire Fire Emblem franchise, the inaugural chapter of FE1 is incredibly successful at incorporating the GAP and learnability framework into the map’s design to effectively teach players mechanics that will be used throughout Fire Emblem’s history. Techniques such as Seeding in the Cutscene, Invisible Hand, and Demonstration of Type are all utilised to implicitly communicate important lessons to the player in spite of the constraints imposed by the size limitations pertinent to game systems of the time.

Fire Emblem Gaiden (FE2)

Released two years after FE1 in 1992, Fire Emblem Gaiden (FE2) was presented as a side-story to its predecessor with an entirely new cast and set on a different continent (Figure 4). Even more so than future titles in the franchise, FE2 experimented heavily with its mechanics and deviated in quite a few ways from its predecessor, including in how the developers abandoned many of the elements that made FE1’s first map successful. While FE1’s map contains varied terrain, including forests, mountains, water, and several different interactable buildings, FE2’s map only makes use of two types of unique tiles: forests and healing tiles. The map is additionally far smaller than that of FE1’s, limiting the player’s ability to make meaningful decisions with movement. Although the predominantly green colour palette highlights the pink healing tiles in the upper left corner, there is no text in the game that explains to the player what they are or how they work. Furthermore, these tiles are placed far enough away that it would take any unit at least two turns to reach them [9] . Given that the goal of this map is to defeat all the enemies, it is unlikely that the player will have any incentive to take the time to explore what these tiles do. In FE2, if an enemy is sufficiently weakened, their AI will switch from prioritising attacking any units in range to reaching the nearest healing tile (11) . However, it is improbable that any player is going to allow a low-HP enemy to escape for several turns to make it all the way to the top left corner and wait to restore HP. Consequently, the placement of these tiles does a poor job at teaching the players what they do and what their role in a map’s design is. Whereas FE1’s first map gives the player access to six classes across seven starting units (and additionally the priest class supplied by Wrys if he is recruited), FE2 only provides three classes spread across five units. Three of the units are villagers, a starting class with poor stats but flexible promotion (Fire Emblem’s manner of upgrading classes) options. The other two units are Alm, a level 1 fighter, and Lukas, a level 2 soldier and the closest equivalent to Jagen on this map. In other words, all five starting units are very functionally similar: they are all foot soldiers who have limited movement capabilities and short-ranged weapons. In comparison, FE1 introduces armoured units (slow with low movement range but high defense), archers (wield ranged weapons such as bows), and pegasus knights (can move over any kind of terrain but weak to bows). Consequently, players are given a much more limited introduction to the types of mechanics they will encounter across the game. This introductory map does make use of Invisible Hand principle in a similar fashion to FE1 where the strongest units have a starting position that results in their max movement range corresponding with the placement of the nearest forest tiles. Thus, players are drawn to placing their units there and naturally discovering how units placed with forests are given avoidance bonuses [10] . As neither of these units are strong enough to fully defeat enemy units in a single turn (barring a lucky critical hit), this positioning is ideal for creating a situation where weaker units can finish foes off for experience points. Consequently, the successes of FE2’s first map are in the places in which it utilises the same design principles as FE1 such as the Invisible Hand. While FE1 makes use of a large but carefully structured design to slowly guide the players through a path that introduces different mechanics consecutively, FE2’s much smaller map struggles to properly communicate how these gameplay systems work. Fire Emblem: The Blazing Sword (FE7)

Fire Emblem: The Blazing Sword (FE7), released internationally for the Game Boy Advance in 2003 as just Fire Emblem, was the first game in the franchise to be published outside of Japan. While FE6, Fire Emblem’s first foray into handheld systems contained an optional tutorial, FE7 marked the first time a Fire Emblem game was released with a dedicated tutorial level. In fact, the first ten chapters of the game, a section known as Lyn Mode (named after the protagonist of these chapters, Lyn), serves as an extended how-to manual for the game’sz mechanics. Every chapter in this introductory section of the game is designed to teach players at least one new aspect of Fire Emblem's systems. However, the design philosophy of Lyn Mode stands in stark contrast to previous games in the series. Upon starting a new game, players are required to complete Lyn Mode before moving onto the bulk of the game’s content in Eliwood Mode. Subsequent playthroughs do provide the option to skip Lyn Mode, however. The map presented in the prologue chapter (Figure 5) is incredibly simple, with only two enemy units and only basic grassland for terrain. Selecting main character Lyn for the first time introduces a new form of communication for Fire Emblem: context-sensitive tutorials. Not only will Lyn comment on what action she should take, but additional windows will pop up to directly instruct players on what to do. These windows also fall under the Just-in-Time Reminder strategy as they only appear when a unit is selected. In this case, the player is taught the very basic action of moving units around the battlefield. Additionally, The Sixth Sense strategy is employed by highlighting exactly which tile the player should move Lyn to.[12] However, the tutorial strictly exerts control over the player’s freedom by preventing them from making any action other than the one dictated by the developers. Should the player attempt to select a different tile, the game will automatically cancel that action.[14] These restrictions persist throughout the map, eventually culminating in an encounter with the boss of the prologue. Here, players are placed in a situation where they would undoubtedly die had the game not included a scripted critical hit during the final clash.15 Surprisingly, the game does not actually draw attention to mechanics behind critical hits, so the player may be left wondering why Lyn dealt extra damage.[15]

Figure 6. Chapter 1: Footsteps of Fate

The second map of Lyn Mode, Chapter 1: Footsteps of Fate (Figure 6), introduces the player not only to two new units, but also intermediate mechanics such as different terrain types and Fire Emblem’s famous weapon triangle advantage system. Once again, the map forces the player to directly follow the developer’s instructions by confining them to predetermined paths. For instance, Chapter 1 introduces the cavalier Sain, and instructs the player to have him attack an axe-wielding opponent using his lance. This is intended to demonstrate the weapon triangle system, where lances are advantageous against swords and weak against axes in that the player either receives a bonus or a penalty to their damage and accuracy depending on what kind of weapon they attack with. Consequently, the player is forced to engage in a scripted encounter where Sain will invariably miss when he attacks [16] , an example of the Demonstration of Action principle. Later, the player is again told to make up for Sain’s prior failing by attacking a different opponent with a sword, only to miss again due to the enemy’s advantage of being hidden in a forest tile.[17] While these lessons do teach the player the consequences of taking certain actions, the repeated instances of negative reinforcement and denying player agency can prove frustrating. Although Andersen et al. (2012) did not find that limiting a player’s freedom had any effect on their enjoyment of a tutorial, FE7’s Lyn Mode is far more expansive and takes at least an hour to complete. Consequently, Lyn Mode is quite poorly regarded among fans of the series with many complaints citing the frustrating experience of not only being deprived of agency for extended periods of time, but also how players are forced to make poor decisions for the tutorial’s story to play out [18] . Correspondingly, Johnson’s paper “Animated frustration or the ambivalence of player agency” (2015) directly examines how the deprivation of player agency can create feelings of frustration in games. For instance, the 2013 game Papers, Please has the players take the role of an immigration officer in the fictional country of Arstotzka. Consequently, players are bound to a cramped desk and required to complete frequent repetitive tasks to simulate the completion of bureaucratic work, all with the knowledge that an authoritarian government is judging any mistakes. As such, frustration is an important part of the game design, as it is meant to invoke the pressures of not only working in the service industry, but also the overwhelming experience of becoming a cog in the authoritarian machine. However, even if the loss of agency is in service of gameplay or story, this does not prevent feelings of frustration from arising and having a negative impact on the player’s experience. In other words, while previous games utilised implicit design methods such as the Invisible Hand (possibly due to space constraints), FE7 instead opts to employ more direct strategies such as the Just-in-Time Reminder, The Sixth Sense, and Demonstration of Action.

However, Lyn Mode’s tendency to remove agency from the player results in feelings of frustration which have impacted the game’s legacy.

Fire Emblem: The Sacred Stones (FE8)

Fire Emblem: The Sacred Stones (FE8) was the third and final title to be released for the Game Boy Advance. While not a direct sequel to its predecessor, FE8 borrows a similar art style and further expands upon FE7’s multiple paths by providing players with a choice between two main characters with differing stories. Most notable, however, is FE8’s introduction of an Easy Mode, a series first. Upon starting a new game in FE8, players are given the option of selecting between an Easy, Normal, and a Difficult mode [19] . While this choice has a significant impact on the design of the entire game, asking the player about their familiarity with game mechanics is in line with the principle of Assessing Prior Knowledge. This principle allows developers to adjust the amount of tutorial present during the game and is often utilised in sequels for games with high replay value. In FE8, selecting Easy mode transforms the prologue map (Figure 7) of the game into a full-fledged guided tutorial, not unlike FE7’s Lyn Mode, that includes the principles of the Just-in-Time Reminder and The Sixth Sense. The map for the prologue chapter is extremely small with the majority of the action taking place within a one tile high path between two mountains. Consequently, players have little choice in where to move their units. This is akin to the concept of Scaffolding, whereby structure is used to help ease players into the game. Traditionally, scaffolding means teaching mechanics through breaking down challenges into smaller steps. In this case, the first map creates bottlenecks to help guide the player towards the goal (defeating the boss), before presenting a much more open level design in Chapter 1 [20] . However, this does result in more limited opportunities for the player to experiment with positioning. Upon entering this map on Easy mode, players start at the bottom right of the map and are required to move main character Eirika to a specific tile towards the top left [21] . The prologue plays out in a very similar fashion to a map in Lyn Mode, where players are forced to follow a very specific set of directions to progress. In contrast, Normal mode starts the player is the upper left of the map and contains no direct instructions [22] . Rather, Normal mode places a greater emphasis on the Seeding in the Cutscene principle to inform players of the actions they should take to complete the map. For instance, the cutscene has Eirika attempting to tend to the wounds of her companion Seth, but he rebuffs her. Consequently, the playable portion of the map starts with Seth at 13/30 HP. Surprisingly, Easy mode does not take this as an opportunity to teach players to use healing items and players are actually locked out of using their inventory due to the scripted nature of the map. Although Seth follows the Jagen archetype in both story role and character design as a paladin, he differs quite heavily from a mechanical standpoint. While Jagen was specifically designed for safely engaging with enemies and setting up for weaker units to gain experience points, Seth is powerful enough to consistently kill all enemies on this map in a single round of combat [23] , including the boss of the prologue [24] . Seth’s dominance continues throughout the game, to the point where he is known as one of the most powerful characters mechanically in the entire franchise. Some players even argue that Seth himself acts as a difficulty modifier [25] , as using him trivialises the majority of content in FE8. In other words, Seth acts as a much more flexible instance of difficulty selection, where players can decide on a per map basis of when and where to utilise him. FE8 marked the introduction of what would become a common feature in future Fire Emblem titles: difficulty selection. Consequently, the designers of FE8’s Easy mode followed in the footsteps of FE7 by borrowing the design philosophy of Lyn Mode to create a very structured tutorial which limits players agency in favour of directly communicating mechanics. Throughout its thirty year history, Fire Emblem has experimented heavily with how to approach tutorial design. As early games were confined by technological constraints, methods had to be devised to communicate lessons implicitly through map design. These games often used methods such as Seeding in the Cutscene, Invisible Hand, and Demonstration of Type to subtly inform players of gameplay elements and how to interact with mechanics. Subsequent hardware improvements allowed future games to include explicit instructions using strategies including the Just-in-Time Reminder, The Sixth Sense, and Demonstration of Action. However, such strategies have had inconsistent implementations, with FE1’s tutorial map being far more fondly remembered than FE2’s inaugural map, despite both games utilising similar approaches. In spite of occasional missteps, succeeding games in the series took advantage of the lessons learned by their predecessors. For instance, the 2017 remake of FE2, the fifteenth game in the franchise titled Fire Emblem Echoes: Shadows of Valentia, would even create an entirely new prologue map that incorporated both implicit and explicit methods to better introduce players to the game’s mechanics. No longer bound by the limitations of technology, Fire Emblem has proven that map design is an incredibly versatile way of tutorialising gameplay and the series has continued to improve in designing effective ways to increase learnability.
