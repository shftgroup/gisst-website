# Japanese Localization

For this technical assignment, I decided to use GISST to compare the starting sequence of Japanese games to their American localizations. It was an interesting process trying to find Japanese games and their American versions since it seems all games in GISST have their original name as the title. For example, I realized I had to search for "Pocket Monsters" instead of "Pokemon" in order to find the Japanese versions. Similarly, I had to find an article that matched Final Fantasy names to their localized counterparts. For example, "Final Fantasy II" in the US is actually "Final Fantasy IV" in Japan since FFII and FFIII in Japan were never released in the US.

Starting with Dragon Quest (Dragon Warrior in the US), the both have different game start sequences before starting the actual gameplay. Aside from the differences in title screen design, hitting "start" in Dragon Quest brings the player directly into a name selection screen before launching the gameplay.[1] On the other hand, hitting "start" in Dragon Warrior[2] brings the player to a "Begin a new quest" screen where the player is prompted to select an "Adventurer Log" or save file before moving into name selection. This slight change likely gives the player a greater feeling of attachment to their gameplay as it directly identifies the player's role in the game.

Comparing the US version of Pokemon Red to the Japanese version (Pocket Monsters Aka) also reveals subtle differences between the different versions of the starting sequence. Perhaps not as noticeable as the difference between the Dragon Warrior and Dragon Quest title screens, the title screen for Pokemon Red shows Red, the protagonist, holding a pokeball;[3] the Japanese version on the other hand, shows Red instead pointing to somewhere in the distance.[4] This difference in character pose on the title screen might signal that the US version, and in turn the audience, will place a greater importance on the act of capturing the pokemon compared to the Japanese version. Another fun point of difference is that interacting with the TV in the room you start in brings up different narration; the US version narrates the player playing the "SNES"[5] while the Japanese version says the player is playing the "Famicon"(ファミコン).[6]
An example of a more noticeable difference in title sequences is seen between the Japanese and US version of Pokemon Stadium on the N64. Where the Japanese version is shorter and more minimal, showing only a handful of pokemon before launching the title screen,[7] the US version has a lot of fanfare with more animations[8] before landing on a different title screen than the Japanese version.

Going back to Dragon Warrior, comparing the opening of Dragon Warrior with the opening of Final Fantasy (the opening for the US and Japanese versions are essentially the same in this case) highlights slightly different focuses between the two games. On the one hand, Dragon Warrior immediately goes to the title screen before prompting the player to create a new save as mentioned above. On the other hand, Final Fantasy goes into a scrolling textblock of exposition before going into party creation where you are able to choose the classes and names of four characters, and then being launched right into the gameplay without any title screen.[9]

I found that, compared to local and cloud emulation, web emulation through GISST was the best way to explore different games since the library came with many games already on it. It being easily accessible on browsers also allows me to easily have multiple windows open simultaneously allowing me to do side-by-side comparisons. Another perk of being able to have side-by-side windows open is when I want to explore a Japanese game but am not necessarily committed to finding a Japanese to English script if I'm only playing the game for a few minutes. In these cases, it has been helpful to have the Japanese version open in one window with the English version open in the other. While the English localization may differ from the exact translation of the Japanese one, having the English version for reference makes it easier to navigate the Japanese game in the case that I want to look at things like art direction or world design where I don't necessarily need the exact translation at the time. One thing that I do miss from web emulation is the ability to play the game with a controller, but I can always boot up local emulation if I feel like playing with a keyboard really hinders a specific game experience. Overall, I think GISST is a wonderful emulation tool and I'm excited to use it for my project.
