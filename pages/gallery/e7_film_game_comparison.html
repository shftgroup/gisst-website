Games: Batman Returns (1993, USA, SNES) and The Adventures of Batman and Robin (1994, USA, SNES) and The Legend of Zelda: Link’s Awakening (USA, Europe)

For technical assignment 3 I examined the adaptations and narrative devices used by the video games, Batman Returns (1993, USA, SNES) and The Adventures of Batman and Robin (1994, USA, SNES). Each game is based on the film/TV series of the same name. Both games were released in the USA on the SNES within two years and feature Batman, so it is possible to examine the narrative conventions employed by the games to align the story with the source material without significant differences in technical possibilities or game structure. Both are sidescrolling beat ‘em up games, a genre that lends itself well to the frequent fighting sequences featured in Batman media.

Batman Returns

Beginning with Batman Returns, a significant amount of effort is taken to incorporate elements of the film of which it is based. The game's opening cutscene shows a 16-bit version of an actual shot from the film with Michael Keaton as Batman and the bat symbol being flashed into his home.

The subsequent animation in the cutscene is also a direct replica from the film, with a shot of the batmobile racing towards the city. There are many other examples of this close attention to detail throughout the game, including the multiple shots of 16-bit versions of the main characters. The main character design, settings, enemies, mini-bosses, and bosses all closely match the film. {% registerCitation "1" "https://gisst.pomona.edu/play/caa1aab5-802e-4585-9a23-f4ff6d7b2136?replay=92e84380-de3b-4389-87d6-df6dd6878d1d" "e7_film_game_comparison" %} The text dialogue in the cut scenes features lines from the film as well. For example, the scene where Batman saves Selina Kyle (Catwoman) from a clown, the cut scene shows a 16-bit replica shot and exact dialogue from the film. For a more concise narrative experience; character motivations, and major plot points are described through scrolling text. Batman’s weapons (specifically his bat-shaped throwing stars) and Batmobile are a significant part of his identity and brand and are appropriately included in the game as well. Due to time constraints and my own lack of gaming prowess, I was only able to complete a bit farther than the first level out of seven, but YouTube play-throughs show that the attention to detail continues throughout the entirety of the game.

The Adventures of Batman and Robin

The Adventures of Batman and Robin, based on the animated children’s TV series of the same name (though the series was titled Batman: The Animated Series for the first season), takes a separate approach from Batman Returns to the adaptation of the source material. The art style of the game is very similar to the TV show, because the show is animated, it is easier to emulate that. There is also some overlap of the music from the show to the game. There is less of a defined narrative in the game The Adventures of Batman and Robin, likely because of the episodic format of the TV show, with shorter, simpler narratives employed as a result. The small story that is portayed in the game is largely told through written dialogue., It is also worth noting that The Adventures of Batman and Robin though still challenging, was not as challenging as Batman Returns. This is likely because the source material is a children’s show as opposed to the PG-13 film. Again, all of these points can be examined further with full play-throughs uploaded to YouTube.

With both adaptations, there is a level of familiarity with the Batman franchise necessary to fully enjoy the experience of the game. Even if the player has not seen the specific film or TV show, the game developers expect players to at the very least be familiar with the main heroes and villians and the setting of Gotham. The more familiar the player is with the specific original source material, the more likely they are to pick up on the details and therefore enjoy the game more. Seriality can be applied here to further explore the Batman franchise and transmedia adaptations as a whole. I plan to dive deeper into the concept of seriality in my final paper.

GISST Review

Of the three technical assignments this semester, I found GISST to be both the least complicated and most enjoyable. All of the configuring has already been done on the back end to set up the emulation of the games, which makes it a simple ‘plug and play’ type of emulation system for the user. The possible drawback to this is the lack of customization available in the choice of games (if they are not already available), game controls, and ability to play in a full screen mode. These are minor issues, though I did run into trouble attempting to play Batman Forever (USA, SNES). The player is required to use a grapple gun to advance to the second floor of the first level, however I could not figure out how to deploy it. In this performance, you can see me attempting to press all of the buttons in all combinations to try to grapple, however I could not figure out which control is associated with that move. The grapple can be seen deployed in this YouTube play-through.

The citation/replay feature is very cool, though it took me some time to understand exactly what the setup was. I thankfully didn’t lose any time to closing a session with unsaved work, since I initially thought that all my save states would be accessible to me even after I closed an instance. Once I understood it I had a lot of fun creating and uploading different moments and replays that might be interesting, and I think there are a few that additionally didn’t make it into the paper. If there is a way to make it so save states can be loaded with a simple button press (perhaps facilitated by RetroArch’s hotkeys?), that would be convenient – I also tried a save corruption glitch which required repeated resets from a save state.

Additionally, the experience of playing a game on GISST is different from playing on the original platform because you can save the game at any point, you can beat levels quicker and play more recklessly knowing that you can restart with no penalty.
