Typically, there are four main “camera” perspectives in early 2-D games: the first-person view (Flight Simulator), the side view (Mario), the top-down view (any early digital chess game), and the isometric view (Qbert). These perspectives were well-established by the time the Nintendo Entertainment System came out, and different gameplay genres quickly adapted specific ones to suit their games: first-person for racing sims and shooters, side views for fighting games and platformers, and so on. However, early role-playing games seemed to move between these perspectives more fluidly, particularly as one starts to draw from both Western and Japanese RPG examples. This essay intends to examine how 2-D RPGs use perspective to inform gameplay and level design within the same genre of games.

1st Person

Perhaps the most well-known series of first-person RPGs, as well as a codifier of many oftheir tropes, is the Wizardry series. Originally released on the Apple II, Wizardry was one of the first attempts to put tabletop role-play game (TTRPG) mechanics into the digital space. In particular, it focuses on the dungeon-delving aspects of many TTRPGs. Because of the original limitations of the system it was developed for, Wizardry is mostly a game of menus and simple visuals. These visuals, however, are where the 1st-person nature of the game is able to make limitations into an immersive experience. The maze explored in most Wizardry games is extremely complex and is able to be so because it only uses a few visual assets to represent the maze.{% registerCitation "1" "https://gisst.pomona.edu/play/caa1aab5-802e-4585-9a23-f4ff6d7b2136?replay=92e84380-de3b-4389-87d6-df6dd6878d1d" "e2_rpg-perspectives" %} The lack of character portraits also means the party creation mechanic can have more complexity because the game doesn’t need to account for visual representations of every variation.

While the 1st-person perspective in RPGs became less of a trend as visual improvements for computer and console games were made, some of Wizardry’s influence can still be seen in games like Earthbound.{% registerCitation "2" "https://gisst.pomona.edu/play/caa1aab5-802e-4585-9a23-f4ff6d7b2136?replay=92e84380-de3b-4389-87d6-df6dd6878d1d" "e2_rpg-perspectives" %} However, most RPGs chose to branch into different perspectives. The most obvious influence on the games discussed in the next two sections is the Ultima series, which uses both grid-based movement and mixture of side and top-down perspectives in various ways.{% registerCitation "3" "https://gisst.pomona.edu/play/caa1aab5-802e-4585-9a23-f4ff6d7b2136?replay=92e84380-de3b-4389-87d6-df6dd6878d1d" "e2_rpg-perspectives" %}

Isometric and Grid-Based

There are few truly isometric RPGs on the SNES, and those that exist are strategy-focused games that take their cues from more traditional strategy games like chess or grid-based combat in tabletop games. As a result, they tend to emphasize the grid as a playing field and focus the majority of their game design on accommodating a flat plane and minimal ways of placing characters in that environment. Of course, a role-play game still has a story and a role, and strategy RPGs (SRPGs) like Fire Emblem are thus forced to weave the narrative aspects of their game into this restrictive grid-based perspective.[4] Where Fire Emblem has focused most of its visual resources is the battle animations, which entirely change the sprite art, environment, and perspective to create a more exciting scene for every clash.[5]

However, taking the isometric route allows for a more fluid and dynamic weaving of story, characters, and gameplay within the same perspective. Tactics Ogre is perhaps the only truly isometric RPG on the SNES and is also an extremely dialogue and story-heavy game. We can see that Tactics Ogre has far more in-depth environments and detailed character sprites than Fire Emblem. While it doesn’t have as complex battle animations, it is able to keep the narrative, gameplay, and animations on a single screen. Establishing narrative shots are done in the game world as opposed to the visual cutscene style of a Fire Emblem narrative introduction.[6] [7] Tactics Ogre also introduced the four-directional system (characters get certain advantages and disadvantages depending on what edge of the square they are facing) and verticality tactics in SRPGs.[8]

Now, there are not necessarily any RPGs that exclusively use the side perspective oftenseen in platformers, beat-em-ups, and fighting games. However, much like the way Tactics Ogre is able to use its perspective and character sprites for both gameplay and narrative, many RPGs created mixed perspectives that allowed for the player to see in a mix of top-down and side- based perspectives. This perspective was likely influenced by the Ultima game series, which introduced a pseudo-top-down way of exploring games in comparison to Wizardry.

An entire genre of RPGs—the Japanese RPG, or JRPG—uses this combination of side and top views as its main perspective. I will show examples from Squaresoft RPGs but its worth noting that other JRPGs of the era like the Tales of series and Ys series make use of the same perspective. We can see that the main advantage of this mixed perspective is that sprites are reusable in both the side-focused battle screen and the top-down exploration screens. In Final Fantasy VI, this is also used on special occasions to play out narrative scenes in a battle screen.[9] Chrono Trigger also used this mixed perspective to give the illusion of verticality in their spaces and dynamic ways to move sprites around in the battlefield.[10]

Conclusions

Generally, the loose nature of what an “RPG” is allows for more experimental ideas within a genre, and the nature of perspective in RPGs is just one example. This is different from genres like fighting games, whose perspective has not changed since the early arcade days, or a genre like first-person shooters where the perspective essentially is the main mechanic. I hope this essay illustrates some of the experimental ways game developers played with computing capabilities in the late 1980s and early 1990s.
