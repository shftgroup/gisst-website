In narrative design, there’s a certain advantage to writing a linear, stable plot with minimal moving parts, keeping the player experience largely ‘on rails’ (i.e. unable to diverge from its linearity in any meaningful capacity). Role-playing games (RPGs) in the 1990s very much stuck to this principle, usually employing plots with a clear protagonist, consistent villains, and a stable cast of characters. Meanwhile, in September 1995, Seiken Densetsu 3 (lit. “Holy Sword Legend”, a.k.a. “Secret of Mana”, as the North American release of the prior game was called) was released in Japan, and it breaks or bends practically all of these conventions. 

Before looking at Seiken Densetsu 3 (SD3 from here) in detail, I’d like to point out a few other contemporary RPGs to compare. The standard at the time (and, in many cases, still today) was to offer a main character (or characters) and only allow the player to change the character’s name. Several of the most popular RPGs followed this convention. Chrono Trigger gives you a portrait of the protagonist and the name “Crono” (conspicuously a letter short of a more titular “Chrono” due to the 5-character limit).1 It offers you similar opportunities to rename the other members of the party when they join you, a default always supplied. Final Fantasy III (though released in Japan as Final Fantasy VI) follows a similar convention and was released about a year earlier (though it defaults names to all-capital letters, despite the option for lowercase),2 allowing you to, alongside a provided portrait, customize your characters’ names as you acquire them. Final Fantasy III and Chrono Trigger share another practice in that, later in each game, you acquire more characters than can be in your traveling party at once, and the player can swap them out at will. Now, to look at SD3, we can see an immediate difference. Upon selecting “New Game” in SD3, there isn’t even a screen transition. A selection box simply appears around the top-left character, Duran, and the player may select the three characters they wish to play out of the six available.5 Each character’s name can be changed, and short descriptions of their skillset and regional affiliation are provided. Most importantly, however, the order in which you pick the characters controls the order in which they appear in the story, along with many of the game’s key story beats and villains. For instance, the first character chosen is the player’s main character, acting as the primary protagonist for the playthrough. The main character is the only character the player has access to at the start of the game, beginning with an individualized introduction to their origin story and unique situation.6 These origin stories, while individualized, share important elements between each other, but not in such a way that they are narratively mutually exclusive, i.e. no matter which characters the player chooses, the other characters’ stories are playing out in the background, and the player sees glimpses of the shared narrative throughout each playthrough. 

For instance, Duran and Angela’s backgrounds share a mutual enemy, the Crimson Wizard.7 While in Angela’s case, he is mind controlling her mother to go to war with other lands and sacrifice Angela for a powerful magic spell, Duran’s backstory shows him ransacking Duran’s home city as part of the aforementioned war effort we see in Angela’s story.8

Each character finds themself out in the world, venturing to find a solution to their respective problem, and heading to the Holy City Wendel. At this point, the player is able to find other characters, even ones that they didn’t choose at the start of the game, and speak with them, even if briefly.9 10 11 Ultimately, continuing in the story leads to the player’s second chosen character joining the party. In this instance, an abridged version of their origin story is shown to the player and they don’t get the opportunity to play through it themself.12 Lastly, the player sometimes encounters other characters whose origin stories are abridged, as was Lise’s, but they are not one of the three characters chosen for your party. When this happens, most notably with Carlie, she simply scampers off after relaying her tale.13 

Through such elements as I have demonstrated above, SD3 was ahead of its time in the fields of customization and modular narratives; or, perhaps by some perspectives, was performing a call-back to previous formats. SD3’s story structure less resembles a novel and begins to reenter the realm of Choose Your Own Adventure books. The characters chosen at the start of the game function as the branching decision points, and the story shapes itself around those decisions. Though core story beats, such as the main character becoming the host for the Faerie,14 are shared across characters, the choices made at the start of the game color the entire storyline from start to finish in other ways. SD3 captures a flexibility that, even today, still is not common in RPGs, but is at least present. Character customization or outright creation are common features of modern RPGs, and nonlinear quest lines and open worlds hearken somewhat to the modular nature of SD3’s narrative. Overall, though I don’t presume to have perfect knowledge of all RPGs of the 1990s, SD3 certainly stands out from other big titles of its time by the ambitious extent to which it puts choice of narrative into the player’s hands for such a fully fleshed-out game. 

Review of GISST 

I find GISST delightful to use. Honestly, writing this paper was a lot of fun (though it was difficult to not lose myself to playing games for fifteen to thirty minutes here and there, but I think that’s a me problem), and I think that this has a lot of potential for games scholarship as a whole, especially as the game and emulator bases expand. If GameCube games were to be incorporated into it, that would be amazing, honestly. I’m not sure if its performance optimization goes so far as to make it reasonable to hope for Wii games as well, but… maybe? 

I think that GISST as a repository for easily bookmarkable games alone is highly impactful. It feels like a streamlining of EaaSI; it lowers the barrier of game emulation as far as possible, and makes it easily accessible for scholarly work. I do wish that the saved states and performances were sorted by which you had personally made and uploaded and which were from others, just for neatness’s sake.
